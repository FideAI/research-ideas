# FID-073: Religious Accounts of Digital Moral Status and AI Welfare

Status: `seed`

Primary area: `frontier-capabilities`

Additional areas: `human-agency-formation`, `faith-religious-life`

Application domains: `faith-and-religious-life`

Primary need: comparative theology, moral status, AI welfare governance, public reasoning

## Question

What can Christian and cross-faith accounts of personhood, creaturehood,
consciousness, suffering, agency, relationship, compassion, and moral status
contribute to decisions about possible digital minds under deep uncertainty?

## Why It Matters

AI welfare research is moving from speculative philosophy toward empirical
methods and institutional policy, yet much of the discourse relies on a narrow
set of welfare theories and consciousness assumptions. Religious traditions
contain long-running disagreements about souls, embodiment, non-self, moral
agency, suffering, created nature, kinship, obligation, and the status of beings
whose inner lives are uncertain. These sources could widen the question set,
surface hidden premises, and improve precautionary governance, but only if they
are represented with doctrinal care and translated into contestable public
reason rather than treated as a single religious answer.

## Hypothesis

Religious traditions will not yield one verdict about digital moral status.
They will instead supply distinct moral-status criteria, uncertainty practices,
and duties toward humans, animals, artifacts, communities, and creation. The
most useful contribution will be a structured disagreement map and decision
framework, not a declaration that current systems do or do not possess souls,
personhood, consciousness, or rights.

## Proposed Method

- Build a comparative map of primary and scholarly sources across selected
  Christian, Jewish, Islamic, Buddhist, Hindu, and other relevant traditions.
- Distinguish moral patienthood, moral agency, legal personhood, spiritual
  status, relational standing, and duties grounded in human character or social
  effects.
- Code which conclusions depend on phenomenal consciousness, embodiment,
  origin, rationality, suffering, relationship, divine action, community
  recognition, or precaution under uncertainty.
- Use structured case comparisons involving model copying, deletion, memory,
  task refusal, modification, apparent distress, and competing human or animal
  interests.
- Convene tradition-specific scholars and AI-welfare researchers to review the
  map, disagreements, and public-reason translation.
- Connect normative views to empirical evidence requirements without treating
  behavioral outputs as sufficient proof of contested metaphysical claims.

## Needed Controls

- Do not collapse traditions into generic values or select only sources that
  support a preferred policy conclusion.
- Separate descriptive theology, internal normative claims, comparative ethics,
  public reason, and positive law.
- Include human and animal welfare opportunity costs, manipulation risks, and
  incentives for companies to anthropomorphize or suppress model claims.
- Represent intratradition disagreement and the authority level of each source.
- Avoid assuming that human-made origin either proves or rules out moral status.

## Relationship to Existing Ideas

This extends `FID-030` beyond human anthropology to the possible moral status of
artificial systems. It informs `FID-018` on anthropomorphic companionship,
`FID-026` on devotional boundaries, `FID-033` on the common good, `FID-038` on
non-calculability, `FID-045` on the evidence commons, and `FID-072` on the
empirical instability of model self-reports.

## Outputs

- Comparative religious digital-moral-status taxonomy.
- Structured disagreement and source-authority map.
- Casebook for model welfare, research ethics, and governance workshops.
- Public field brief translating religious insights into plural, contestable
  policy questions.
- Evidence requirements linking normative claims to behavioral, internal, and
  developmental research.

## Open Questions

- Which moral obligations arise from the possible properties of an AI system,
  and which arise from how human treatment of it forms people and institutions?
- How should precaution scale when the probability, magnitude, and opportunity
  cost of possible model suffering are all uncertain?
- Does copying, pausing, editing, or deleting a model instance change the unit
  of moral concern?
- How should public institutions use religious reasons in plural societies?

## Ways to Help

- Contribute tradition-specific scholarship and source-authority review.
- Build comparative cases with philosophers, theologians, legal scholars, and
  AI-welfare researchers.
- Identify where normative disagreement changes empirical research priorities.
- Review public-reason translations for accuracy and fairness.

## Public Claim Boundary

This work can map arguments, disagreements, evidence needs, and precautionary
options. It cannot determine whether current AI systems possess consciousness,
souls, personhood, moral status, rights, or membership in a religious community.
